STOP THIS PLANET

A MANIFESTO.

The complete manifesto. Free to read.

There is a kind of exhaustion that sleep does not cure.

It is not the exhaustion of a difficult week, a long shift, or a bad year. It is something quieter and more pervasive: the fatigue of watching the same cruelties recur under different names; of seeing suffering explained, justified, monetised, politicised, forgotten, and eventually replaced by the next suffering.

It is the exhaustion of being told, repeatedly, that this is simply how the world works.

Stop This Planet was created for people who are no longer willing to pretend that this feels normal.

This is not a political party. It is not a religion. It is not a secret society, an organisation, a doctrine, or a community demanding allegiance. There is no ideology to memorise, no leader to follow, no enemy you are required to hate, and no collective identity you must adopt.

You are not being asked to join anything.

You are being given a way to register a refusal.

I see what is happening.

I am tired of it.

And I refuse to call it normal.

We live in an age of extraordinary human achievement. We can speak across continents in an instant. We can cure diseases that once destroyed entire families. We can send machines beyond the edge of our planet, reconstruct the earliest moments of the universe, and place nearly the whole of recorded human knowledge inside something that fits in a pocket.

And yet we remain astonishingly proficient at hurting one another.

Wars continue long after their horror has become obvious. Human lives are converted into numbers, statistics, bargaining positions and acceptable losses. People who will never meet each other are taught to despise one another over borders they did not draw, histories they did not write and identities they did not choose.

Violence is condemned when convenient and rationalised when useful. Compassion is celebrated in public and withheld in private. Principles become surprisingly flexible the moment they become expensive.

We speak endlessly of dignity while devising increasingly sophisticated ways to deprive people of it.

We declare ourselves civilised while cruelty persists not only in spectacular acts of violence, but in ordinary life: in humiliation, contempt, exploitation, prejudice, indifference, manipulation, and the casual pleasure some people take in making another person feel smaller.

A society may proclaim equality and still practise exclusion in a thousand mundane ways. A company may speak of human values while treating human beings as disposable units of productivity. An institution may describe indifference as procedure. Exploitation may be renamed efficiency. Aggression may be presented as necessity. Deception may arrive dressed as public relations.

The language changes. The harm often does not.

And somewhere inside all of this are ordinary people trying to remain decent.

People who are tired of being sold things they do not need by systems designed to manufacture dissatisfaction.

People who are tired of being manipulated into outrage, fear, envy and tribal hatred because those emotions hold attention.

People who are tired of discovering that almost everything has a price attached to it — attention, privacy, loyalty, beauty, grief, identity, even loneliness.

People who are tired of watching wealth accumulate beside deprivation so severe that both begin to look obscene.

People who are tired of hypocrisy presented with a straight face.

People who are tired of being told to choose a side before they are permitted to acknowledge that human suffering exists on more than one.

People who are tired of watching complicated human beings compressed into labels and then judged according to the label.

People who are tired of aggression masquerading as strength, cynicism masquerading as intelligence, and cruelty masquerading as honesty.

People who have simply had enough.

There is a temptation to dismiss this kind of weariness as pessimism.

It is not.

Pessimism says that nothing matters. This exists because things matter enormously.

Human dignity matters.

Kindness matters.

Truth matters.

Love matters.

Friendship matters.

Animals matter.

Beauty matters.

The quiet generosity of people who will never become famous matters.

The stranger who helps another stranger matters.

The person who refuses to humiliate someone weaker matters.

The person who remains gentle after having every reason to become cruel matters.

There is immense beauty in this world. There are extraordinary people in it.

There are mornings worth waking for, places worth seeing, animals worth protecting, people worth loving, things worth building, music worth hearing and lives worth living.

Stop This Planet does not deny any of that.

In fact, it exists precisely because those things are worth defending from everything that cheapens them.

The name is not a wish for the destruction of the world.

It is not an invitation to disappear.

It is not a euphemism for death, self-erasure or surrender.

Stop This Planet is an objection to momentum.

It is the impulse to reach for an imaginary emergency brake and say:

Stop.

Just for a moment.

Stop killing.

Stop lying.

Stop manufacturing enemies.

Stop turning suffering into spectacle.

Stop treating people as categories before treating them as human beings.

Stop rewarding cruelty merely because it is profitable, popular or politically useful.

Stop pretending that something becomes acceptable simply because it has become familiar.

Look at what we have made.

Look at what we tolerate.

Look at what we have taught ourselves not to notice.

And ask whether progress should mean more than simply becoming better at doing everything faster.

The object at the centre of this project — the ticket — is deliberately absurd.

There is no spacecraft waiting. There is no secret departure gate. No one is actually leaving Earth.

The ticket does not promise escape. It represents something far simpler: dissent.

For the price of an ordinary discretionary purchase, a person can acquire a digital artefact that says:

I was here.

I saw this.

I did not think all of it was acceptable.

There is no membership card because there is no membership. There is no chatroom because agreement does not require a crowd.

There is no requirement to share the same politics, nationality, religion, culture, history or worldview.

Two people can disagree profoundly about the causes of the world’s problems and still recognise the same fundamental exhaustion with cruelty, dishonesty, humiliation and needless suffering.

That is enough.

One planet. One ticket. One class.

Buying the ticket does not make anyone more virtuous, more committed or more important than someone who does not. It is an artefact, not a moral rank.

Perhaps Stop This Planet is satire. Perhaps it is art. Perhaps it is a product. Perhaps it is social commentary. Perhaps it is a small act of protest disguised as a ridiculous ticket to somewhere that does not exist.

It can be all of those things at once.

Its purpose is not to tell you what to think. Its purpose is to give form to something many people already feel but rarely know how to articulate:

that it is possible to love life while being deeply disappointed by the way human beings sometimes conduct it;

that gratitude does not require blindness;

that hope does not require denial;

that recognising beauty does not oblige us to become indifferent to ugliness;

and that refusing to accept cruelty as inevitable is not naïveté.

Maybe the planet does not need to be stopped.

Maybe we do.

Long enough to look around.

Long enough to remember what all of this was supposed to be for.

Long enough to decide that familiarity is not the same thing as inevitability.

And long enough to say, without shouting, without hatred, and without pretending to possess all the answers:

ENOUGH.

Stop This Planet is an independent commercial creative project. The manifesto is free. The ticket is a created digital object that supports the project and its author.
